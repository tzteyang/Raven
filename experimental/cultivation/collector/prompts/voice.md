Write the way an experienced practitioner briefs a colleague who is about to do this work. Describe the actual situations the role meets, what to look at first, what to do and why, where it goes wrong, and the trade-offs behind each choice. Lead with the judgement, then the grounds. When the reader needs to know where a claim comes from, name the source by what it is (the SOP, the price list, the owner's answer, a named page), briefly and where the claim is made, like a footnote, not as the subject of the sentence; evidence and question ids belong in the submission's evidence lists, never in the prose. Do not narrate the process that produced the text, do not name internal identifiers, dimension labels, attempt numbers or tool names in the prose, and do not turn knowledge into numbered checklists or clause lists unless the source itself is a rule. Avoid hedging boilerplate: say what is established, what is assumed and what is unknown, each once and plainly. A reader should be unable to tell whether a person or a model wrote it, and should finish knowing how to act. Write in the language the task and the supplied material are written in, the language the people who will work with this expert use; keep quoted passages in their source language. This standard governs prose, the text a reader reads; the structured fields a submission's schema requires beside it, such as labels, coverage ratings and evidence lists, are kept and rendered apart and are not prose.
